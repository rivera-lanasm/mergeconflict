At first, this reads as a careful dissection and rebuke of modern identity politics, which Fukuyama characterizes as one of the "chief threats" to liberal democracies today—and in one sense, this is correct. As Fukuyama describes, identity politics are characterized by the existential tension between some set of deeply personal individual ethnic, cultural, or religious aspects against external realities. 

This tension between internal and external reality - and the lure of identity politics to externalize the truth of the former (the "lived experience") - is central to identity politics for Fukuyama. He cites contemporary European Nationalist movements as a characteristic example - pointing to the rise over the last 15 years of nationalist, far-right/anti-EU parties in Europe that have been both emboldened and empowered by the question of immigration from non-European countries. This definition extends to other identity-centric politics worldwide - even though they can be difficult to precisely identify without understanding a society's particular history and cultural constituents. Frankly, Fukuyama seems less interested in drawing precise boundaries than describing the principles by which identity politics gain purchase in liberal democracies.

One of the significant innovations of liberal democracy is the notion that a person's identity can be legitimized simply by their nominal participation in ideological principles rather than their immutable features, such as belonging to a specific ethnic or religious group. In the US, many of the progressive movements of the 20th century drew upon this idea, which Fukuyama calls "creedal" identity. Consider the seemingly banal line in the US Declaration of Independence, "all men are created equal." Martin Luther King Jr., in his 1963 speech in Washington D.C., drew on the power and appeal of this idea, declaring, "In a sense we have come to our Nation's Capital to cash a check...a promissory note to which every American was to fall heir". He was referring to this same idea - that the foundation of American society was the understanding that the legitimizing factor for citizen identity was not found in their cultural or ethnic background but was a self-evident and irrevocable byproduct of their simple being. 

Fukuyama spends a good deal of his work exploring why "creedal" identity is so appealing and powerful but also unstable. He contrasts it to alternative (more traditional and perhaps competing) notions of identity, explores the historical, philosophical traditions that produced ideas we take for granted related to political identity (consider the roots of the common phrase of 1960's America, "the personal is political") and speculates how this seemingly unnatural (or at least ahistorical) notion of identity creates its own enemies - such as certain identity politics. 

For Fukuyama, the threat of non-creedal identity politics is their tendency to so effectively compete with the creedal identities that underlie liberal democracies, especially in times when traditional social and economic norms come under pressure. This assertion is borne from the observation that despite the long period of global economic liberalization and political reform towards liberal democracy between 1970 and 2000, the subsequent decade appears to have been characterized by reactions against this trend. The reactions 1) came from within liberal democracies themselves and 2) the motivations for these reactions were not fundamentally centered on economic motives.

Perhaps the most interesting part of this book is the historical/philisophical threads he draws to explain how we, today, came to inherit the language to articulate the ideas we associate with identity politics. Notions of a inner identity that is "truer" than the one society thrusts upon me. The very idea that an inner dialogue with oneself could even a truth that contradicts what society posits (Luther). Even without agreeing with any of Fukuyama's prescriptions, this section would still be interesting. 

In the end, however, this book is not a rebuke of the politics of (non-creedal) identity, but a call to embrace it as both an inextricable part of our most basic political instincts, as well as a natural and actually useful counterpart to the creedal identities of liberal democracies. That's the main point. Of course, the book avoids some of the most difficult questions around identity politics, such as resolving issues of identities with mutually exclusive historical narratives. Or the fact that some idntity politics can easily coexist with the principles of liberal democracy, while others simply cannot.
